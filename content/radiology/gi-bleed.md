# Definitions

- Upper GI bleed = proximal to ligament of Treitz (oesophagus, stomach, duodenum)

- Lower GI bleed = distal to ligament of Treitz 

## Small print

- Small bowel bleed = distal to ampulla of Vater, proximal to ileocaecal valve

- Suspected small bowel bleed = no bleeding source identified on OGD and colonoscopy

- Obscure GI bleed = no bleeding source identified after endoscopy and imaging

- Overt GI bleed = visible (haematemesis, haematochezia, malaena)

- Occult GI bleed = not clinically visible (anaemia, faecal occult blood)

- Massive GI bleed = haemodynamic instability

  
# Clinical presentation

UGIB: haematemesis, maelana (haematochezia if brisk)
LGIB: haematochezia

# Investigations
CTA before endoscopy or invasive angiography for suspected LGIB if 
- haemodynamically unstable
- shock index > 1 after resuscitation (HR > SBP)
- active bleeding suspected
Reasons: quick, no bowel prep or sedation needed, no evidence for benefit of endoscopy before CTA

Factors predicting positive CTA (appendix from ACG and SAR consensus guidelines)
- recent bowel resection
- antiplatelets
- more than 3 units packed red cells per day
- tachycardia
- hypotension
- haematochezia within last 4 hours

Negative CTA needs lower GI endoscopy +/- upper GI endoscopy

Suspected UGIB needs endoscopy as initial investigation (NICE)

Post-polypectomy straight to endoscopy



# Imaging
## Technique

### Overt bleeding
To identify active bleed
- Noncontrast to identify intraluminal hyperdense material (faeces, oral contrast, pills) and foreign bodies which may mimic contrast extravasation 
- Late arterial (10 s after aortic bolus trigger, or 25 - 35 s after injection) for extravasation, arterial anatomy, vascular lesions (e.g. AVM), masses e.g. NET
- Late venous (70 - 90 s after injection)

No neutral oral contrast (will dilute extravasated contrast)

### Occult bleeding
To identify potential causes
Variable protocols which may include enteric phase (50 s), portal venous phase (60 - 70 s)

## Features
- Hyperdense intraluminal fluid on noncontrast images (simple fluid 0 - 15 HU, unclotted blood 30 - 45 HU, clotted blood 45 - 70 HU)
- Sentinel clot (acute clotted blood, hyperdense, closest to bleeding site)
- Active bleed = contrast extravasation (arterial phase; venous phase if venous bleeding or slow/low-volume arterial bleeding), which changes in size, shape, attenuation (venous phase)
  
### Mimics of active haemorrhage
- Hyperdense intraluminal material, foreign bodies (present on noncontrast)
- Mucosal hyperenhancement (diseased bowel wall, normal collapsed bowel)
- Vascular lesions (no change in size, shape and attenuation on venous phase, follows blood pool)
- Cone beam artefact (hyperdensity at air-fluid interfaces)

# Causes
## Upper GI bleeding

### Peptic ulcer disease
- Ulcer = focal outpouching, defect in mural enhancement

### Oesophagitis, gastritis, duodenitis
- Suspected reflux oesophagitis if distal oesophageal thickening + hiatus hernia

### Cameron lesions
- Ulcers due to large hiatus hernia (sliding or pressure). Suspect if large hiatus hernia and no radiological bleed.

### Variceal bleeding

### Mallory-Weiss syndrome

### Neoplasm

### Dieulafoy lesions
- Submucosal artery eroding through a small mucosal defect, usually in proximal lesser curvature of the stomach
- Arterially enhancing nidus, not well seen on enteric or venous phase (unless bleeding)

### Roux-en-Y gastric bypass
- Early: anastomoses, gastric pouch staple line
- Late (> 30 days postop): marginal ulceration near gastrojejunostomy anastomosis

## Lower GI bleeding

### Diverticulosis
- Painless haematochezia
- Most common in left and sigmoid colon, but if bleeding more common in right colon

### Angiodysplasia
- Punctate or discoid foci of mural enhancement (enteric phase)

### Colonic ischaemia
- Sudden-onset crampy abdominal pain, onset of haematochezia or bloody diarrhoea within 24 hours

### Anorectal disease
- Haemorrhoids
- Rectal varices (look like haemorrhoids but above dentate line and with other features of portal hypertension)
- Rectal ulcers
- Radiation proctitis

### Neoplasm

## Small bowel bleeding
### Small bowel ulcers
- Usually not visible on CT, indirect signs only (inflammation)

### Crohn disease
- Can involve multiple segments, strictures preclude endoscopy, increased risk of recurrent bleeding

### NSAID enteropathy
- Multiple short-segment (diaphragm-like) strictures, clustered within same segment, usually ileum
- Mimics include Crohn disease, radiation-induced strictures, cryptogenic multifocal ulcerous stenosing enteritis

### Dieulafoy lesions
- Usually stomach but 16% in small bowel (usually duodenum)
- Highest enhancement in arterial phase (like AVMs but no draining vein)

### Arteriovenous malformations
- Arterially enhancing nidus with enlarged or early enhancing draining vein

### Angiodysplasia
- Not well seen in arterial phase, highest enhancement in enteric or portal venous phase
- Most commonly in jejunum

### Venous lesions
- Varices, venous malformations, haemangioma


### Neoplasm
- NET (plaque-like then U-shaped, desmoplastic reaction), adenocarcinoma (stricturing), GIST (exophytic), lymphoma (aneurysmal), metastasis
